The thesis

Writing these down, a few things stood out. A lot had to go right even for the small things to happen. And in almost every scenario the baseline looked like luck, but some of it was engineered. I got into physics because I picked up the wrong book at a bookshop, and that one was pure accident. Most of the rest was me trying to increase the surface area of luck, so that situations landed near me and I was prepared to take advantage of them when they did. I did something interesting and met interesting people every summer of college because I was open to going out of my way, reaching out to people, showing more interest than the situation asked for. The week I decided I wanted to switch fields, I was talking to four professors, had applied for a bunch of internships, and was half a textbook in. So honestly things do happen when we just decide to do it. Same reason I flew west around the planet last year, because it was the only legal way to get east to a client.

Four things I want the personal posts to carry

  • Becoming the most formidable version of myself (Paul Graham vocab for the most desirable quality in a founder). That is how I would like to think about myself, and it is what I am working toward.
  • The just-do-it mentality. You can literally just do things. Switch fields, send the email, get on the flight. Most people wait for permission that is not coming. We all know exactly what to do and still spend the time looking for what to do instead.
  • Luck is not random. You cannot manufacture it, but you can widen the surface it lands on, and almost everything above is that.
  • And above all, do not take yourself too seriously. Most of these will lean funny and light-hearted rather than serious. Half the stories are me being an idiot who got lucky, and that is the part worth telling. Nobody needs another LinkedIn post about resilience.

All of this is setup. From mid-August I start posting about the SF trip itself, the prep and then the move, and that runs as a series.

03

How the posts are built

Real artifacts on screen. Sent-mail folders, the actual reply, boarding passes, manuscripts, bad generated samples, the book itself.
Ugly beats flattering. A blurry 2016 photo carries more credibility than anything shot well.
One scene, not a narrative arc. The bookshop counter. The Sunday morning the email landed. Detail does the work.
Be the idiot in the story, not the hero. Self-deprecation is what buys the seriousness later.
Never state the lesson. The post ends when the story ends, no closing aphorism.
Nothing staged now to look like then. If a story has no artifact, it runs without one or waits.
Crop hard on anything with other people in it. Friends and clients did not sign up for my LinkedIn.
Cadence: one or two personal posts a week against three technical. Technical carries credibility, personal carries memory.
