Opinion By Assem Chammah 8 randomised trials 55 school systems 50 years of tools in class September 2026 . The short version. The debate is framed as "climb or fly". A Fields medallist says AI is a helicopter that drops children on the summit. NVIDIA's CEO says the skills they lose may not matter.. Both accept the same frame. A fixed mountain, and a choice between walking and flying.. The evidence says design decides. Plain ChatGPT cut exam scores by 17% in one trial. The same model, set up to give hints instead of answers, cost nothing on the exam.. Schools lag every step change. Ten years after maths teachers endorsed calculators, 47% of US 4th graders had never been asked to use one in class.. The bottleneck is the adult. 92% of UK students use AI. 9% of French teachers were trained on it last year.. Hints are the floor, not the goal. A tutor that only gives hints stops the loss. The prize is using AI to reach work students could never reach alone.. My answer: train the pilots, starting with the teachers. Spend less school time on execution and more on judgment. Then raise the summit.. . This month, two very different voices crossed my feed.. The mathematician. The first was Hugo Duminil-Copin, Fields Medal 2022, on France Inter. He was promoting De l'autre côté du tableau noir , the book he wrote with Geneva psychologist Emmanuel Sander.. His image: letting AI solve a maths problem for a child is like asking a helicopter to drop you at the summit.. His verdict: nice photo, zero effort. The only thing the child learns is how to get into a helicopter.. The echo: La Libre Belgique used the same image on 18 September: being airlifted to the top of Everest instead of walking up. 1 . The CEO. The second was Jensen Huang, CEO of NVIDIA.. The finding put to him: children who lean on AI get their homework done faster but learn less.. His answer: he agreed. Long division, multiplication tables and square roots are being forgotten.. His follow up: does it matter? Probably not those skills, he said. We will discover new ones.. Where I land. I think both are partly right, and both miss the point. I spent years on school improvement projects at McKinsey and a good part of my career since building AI. Here is my position:. The helicopter is not the problem. Flying one is far harder than walking. The problem is that we still grade children on the walk.. The bar is now too low, not too high. With AI in every pocket, the mountain we ask children to climb should be much higher.. Education systems are structurally bad at step changes. They were slow on calculators and slow on the internet. AI is a bigger step than both, and it is arriving faster.. The binding constraint is teachers, not students. Children will use AI whatever we decide. The question is whether the adult in the room can fly better than they can.. The rest of this piece backs each point with data: eight randomised trials, surveys of teachers in 55 school systems, and fifty years of history.. 2 The mathematician's case: the value is in the climb. The argument deserves to be steelmanned before it is challenged.. Who is speaking. Hugo Duminil-Copin , born 1985, is a French probabilist at the University of Geneva and the IHES. 2 . The Fields Medal , on 5 July 2022, rewarded his work on phase transitions in models such as percolation and the Ising model. 3 . His new book with Emmanuel Sander (Seuil, 2026) attacks the myth that mathematics is reserved for a handful of geniuses. 4 Behind the rigour of theorems, it argues, sit intuition, analogy, trial and error, emotion and productive mistakes.. Sander researches mental representations and how learners interpret problems.. What he says. In the France Inter interview, his core points are:. Transferable capability, not the answer. Today's mountain is the exercise. There will be another tomorrow. School exists to equip children for mountains they have not seen yet.. AI short circuits the core of mathematics: building concepts and tools yourself, and finding your own way to a solution.. Mathematicians produce tomorrow's intuitions. Zero and negative numbers were once frontier ideas. Today they are common sense.. His own practice. He told Le Monde he does not use AI in his research. The host called that almost anachronistic, given how widely the French now use AI.. He is not alone. La Libre reports that Mickaël Randour, head of mathematics at UMONS, signed the Leiden Declaration, a warning from mathematicians about AI misuse in research. 1,5 . His point: AI proofs written in the formal language Lean can be checked as correct without anyone understanding why they are true. Humanity learns nothing reusable from them.. His rule: the brain is a muscle, and mental arithmetic comes before the calculator.. The strongest version of the argument. It is not nostalgia. It is that understanding is built through productive struggle, and a tool that removes the struggle can remove the learning with it.. That concern is real. Section 4 shows it is partly supported by data.. The counter voice in the same article. Amaury Hayat, a mathematician working on AI for proofs, disagrees. 1 . His view: doing maths with only pen and paper no longer makes much sense, and researchers who refuse AI will struggle to find posts.. His metaphor: AI for a proof is less a helicopter ride than climbing Everest in a robotic exoskeleton instead of with an ice axe. Even an assisted ascent teaches you the route.. 3 The CEO's case: some skills stop mattering. The context. Jensen Huang spoke on The Ezra Klein Show (New York Times, 23 September 2026). 6 Klein opened with a large Chinese study on AI in school and asked Huang what he made of it.. The detail nobody quotes: most of the loss came from what the authors call homework outsourcing, very short homework time with very high homework scores. A school that still grades take home work is reading a broken gauge.. What Huang said. He agreed with the finding. Long division, multiplication tables and square roots are being forgotten.. He asked whether it matters, and answered that he did not think it does. Some skills matter, he conceded, but society will discover new ones.. His illustration: he does not know his own address, ZIP code or phone number, and once panicked at a petrol pump when asked for his ZIP code.. After pushback from Klein, he framed the trade as losing some fine intellectual dexterity while becoming better systems thinkers. 8 . Why I agree with the direction, with a grain of salt. The direction is right. Every generation offloads skills to tools. Nobody mourns the slide rule or the log table, and nobody memorises phone numbers any more.. The messenger is conflicted. NVIDIA sells the hardware that powers AI adoption. Discount the enthusiasm accordingly.. The example is weak. Forgetting a ZIP code is not the same as never learning number sense. You cannot become a systems thinker without a mental model of the system, and you cannot spot a wrong AI answer without a feel for what a right one looks like.. "We will discover new skills" is half a plan. He is right that new skills matter more than the old ones. The main one is already clear: judgment. Naming it and teaching it now is the job of education systems. Discovering it a generation late is the failure mode.. 4 What the evidence says: design decides the outcome. The research does not say "AI harms learning". It says unstructured AI harms learning, and structured AI can accelerate it. Each camp quotes half of it.. Three terms, explained once. Randomised controlled trial (RCT). Students are assigned by lottery to use a tool or not. Any difference in results can then be credited to the tool.. Effect size, in standard deviations (SD). A common ruler for learning gains across studies. 0.2 SD is roughly a student moving from the middle of the class to the top 42%; 0.4 SD, to the top 34%.. Guardrailed tutor. An AI set up to teach, not to answer: it gives hints one step at a time and never hands over the full solution.. Same model, opposite outcome. The most cited 'ChatGPT boosts learning' number no longer exists. Field trials that measure learning without the tool report far smaller, and far more useful, numbers.. The OECD's 2026 Digital Education Outlook names the problem directly: meta analyses claiming ChatGPT boosts "academic performance" often measure immediate task achievement, "not durable learning, and sometimes not even learning". 10 . Three conclusions I draw. The helicopter without a pilot licence is real. When children use a general chatbot to produce answers, measured learning drops. Duminil-Copin is right about that.. The tool is not the variable. The pedagogy is. Guardrailed tutors, sequencing (think first, then AI) and teacher framing reverse the effect. Huang's "it doesn't matter" ignores this. So does the mathematician's "don't use it".. The biggest gains came with an adult in the loop. Nigeria, Harvard, Stanford and the Google and Eedi trial all had educators designing or supervising the experience. That is the thread I pull on below.. So what? Banning AI and handing it out unstructured are the two worst options on the table. The winning option costs the most thought: a tool built to teach, used in an order a teacher designed. And that only protects the floor. Section 8 is about the ceiling.. 5 We have seen this film before. Each time a tool made the old exercise trivial, schools first resisted, then slowly raised the bar. The lag is the damage.. Calculators: endorsed in 1980, tested in 1994. Ownership came first. By 1975, a Science News estimate put one calculator in circulation for every nine Americans. 11 By 1980, 75% of US 9 year olds and 85% of 17 year olds owned or had access to one. 12 . The profession said yes early. In 1980, the NCTM's Agenda for Action recommended calculators at all grade levels. 11 Nearly 100 studies had found no measurable harm. 13 . Classrooms did not follow. In 1986, some teachers picketed the NCTM meeting in Washington against its pro calculator position. 14 The famous photo overstates it: about 15 of 6,500 attendees took part. 15 The real resistance was quieter, and it lived in daily practice.. Ten years later, it had still not landed. The 1990 NAEP found 47% of 4th graders and 22% of 8th graders were never asked to use a calculator in maths class. Only 2% of 4th graders could use one on a test. The US statistics agency's own verdict: "NCTM's recommendations have not been implemented." 16 . The exam moved last. The SAT allowed calculators from March 1994, fourteen years after the endorsement. 87% of test takers brought one that year. 11 . hover or tap a year 1975 One calculator for every nine Americans 11 . 1980 NCTM recommends calculators at all grade levels 13 . 1983 AP Calculus allows calculators, then reverses the next year 11 . 1986 Teachers picket the NCTM meeting 15 . 1990 47% of 4th graders never asked to use one in class 16 . 1994 The SAT allows calculators; 87% bring one 11 . . . A caveat first. I have spent years reading on education and worked on school improvement projects during my time at McKinsey. What follows is my personal view, not a conclusion of that work.. I would take both Duminil-Copin and Huang with a grain of salt. One is a researcher at the absolute frontier of his field, with a way of working that suits him and very few others. The other sells the shovels of this gold rush.. The metaphor argues against its author. Flying a helicopter is harder than walking. Ask someone who has never flown to land on Everest: thin air, unpredictable winds, a landing zone the size of a table. It is one of the hardest things a pilot can do. Getting into the helicopter is not the skill. Flying it is.. The child in his story is a passenger, not a pilot. That is the real failure. Not that a helicopter exists, but that we let children ride in it without teaching them to fly, navigate, read the weather or know when to land.. If the climb is now easy, the mountain is too small. The right response to a helicopter is not to ban it. It is to pick a higher mountain, or a range nobody has mapped.. Amaury Hayat's exoskeleton makes the same point from inside the profession: an assisted ascent still teaches you the route, if you are paying attention.. Where I agree with Huang, and where I go further. Huang is right on the direction. Some skills stop mattering, and new ones take their place. The most important new skill already has a name: judgment.. Execution is getting cheap. Computing, retrieving, drafting and formatting are what AI does well. They used to fill most of a school day.. Judgment is getting valuable. Framing the problem, choosing the method, checking the answer, deciding what good looks like: that is what is left for people.. School still trains execution. Too much time goes to learning by heart and executing procedures mindlessly. That was never the point of education, and now a machine does it in seconds.. Some execution stays. You cannot judge whether an AI's answer is plausible without number sense, or direct it to write a strong argument if you have never built one. But a skill that used to be manual does not have to stay manual.. Keep the execution that judgment rests on, and hand the rest to the tool. What changes is how much time we spend on execution versus judgment, and how high we set the bar once the tool is in hand. Train teachers and students in judgment, not in mindless execution.. The skills the job market already names. Employers are not waiting. In the World Economic Forum's 2025 survey, employers expect 39% of workers' core skills to change by 2030, and seven in ten companies list analytical thinking as essential. 24 Those are pilot skills, not passenger skills.. 8 Hints are the floor. The summit is the point.. Read section 4 quickly and you could draw the wrong lesson: lock the model down so it only gives hints. That protects learning. It does not raise it.. What hints do, and what they do not. They stop the loss. In the Turkey trial, the hint only tutor left exam scores where they were without AI. 9 No loss. Also no gain.. They free time.
