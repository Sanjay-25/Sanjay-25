We put "We Must Pace the Frontier" to 4,491 simulated people in three societies: Tech Twitter (1,884), US tech leaders (1,526) and the US public (1,081). They back the oversight in the plan and reject the slowing down. Read post : We Accurately Simulated Misinformation, Fact-Checking, and Prebunking Experiments . . . . . September 2026 . AI Researchers Should Use Group Alignment to Reduce P(doom). Emmanuelle Gelain-Sohn, Yitian Chen, Felix Wallis. . Frontier AI no longer involves individual agents working as lone savants to reach a eureka moment. Instead, the reality looks closer to large groups, sometimes 10,000 or more, working together to reach an insight, be it about mathematics or committing cybercrimes. Techniques to align individual agents, such as RLHF, provide no guarantees that they will compose safely in these collaborative environments, and the AI community generally lacks theoretical and empirical tools to describe alignment in these settings. To address these problems, this blog post proposes a definition of group alignment that distinguishes it from single-model alignment. It then outlines several numerical experiments that demonstrate how practitioners can group-align models using a technique called ‘mixed swarms’. We conclude with next steps for safety research on model collectives and call on fellow practitioners in academia and industry to help address the risks associated with artificial collective intelligence.. Synthetic research often creates an illusion of precision, since matching top-level survey distributions masks incoherent internal belief systems in AI personas. To solve this, true simulation accuracy requires an end-to-end validity framework that grounds persona stability, latent psychometric traits, and human-calibrated consistency against real-world causal effects. While standard providers typically evaluate models on two benchmarks, we believe rigorous enterprise testing requires eight validity criteria to ensure reliable predictions for high-stakes decisions.. Consequential Behaviour and Representational Fairness in the Validation of Synthetic Research. Kutzner, F., Kacperski, C., de Molière, L., Chidichimo, E., Jung, M. J., Wallis, F. P. S., & He, J. K. · arXiv Preprint. . Researchers in industry and academia use synthetic survey respondents powered by large language models as substitutes for human samples. These synthetic populations require validation against real-world data, so researchers often address them using ad hoc comparisons with human surveys. Inspired by the intention-behaviour gap in behavioural science, we argue that these validations test the wrong thing for most applied cases where decision makers commission synthetic research to anticipate consequential behaviour. To address this problem, we propose a validation framework with two requirements. First, every validity claim must state its level of correspondence with human data: does the sample predict what the represented people do, which of four diagnostics (location, dispersion, response process and structure) does the validation address, and does the validation compare against experimental effects? Second, researchers must report validity claims for subgroups, since these groups are often the most affected by consequential decisions and aggregate accuracy hides their misrepresentation. Our validation framework operationalises three justice dimensions (distributional, procedural, and recognition) as measurable quantities and defines within-persona counterfactual experiments as a validation requirement. We then apply the framework to electric vehicle charging tariffs, before closing with a reporting checklist that researchers can use to make convincing validity claims. Read more . I., Mediano, P. A. M., Leong, V., Dumas, G., Canales-Johnson, A., & Bethlehem, R. A. I. · Nature Reviews Neuroscience. . Human social interaction depends on coordination across language, movement, physiology, affect, and brain activity. In this Nature Reviews Neuroscience Perspective, the authors propose a methodological paradigm shift for social neuroscience; namely, that social neuroscience needs a unified information-theoretic framework to study these interpersonal dynamics. By bringing together mutual information, transfer entropy, and information decomposition, the framework gives researchers a common statistical language for nonlinear, directional, and higher-order social dynamics. This framework gives researchers a more robust way to compare evidence across levels of analysis, test mechanisms of coordination, and explain how people influence one another in real time.. Chidichimo, E. · Encyclopedia of Religious Psychology and Behavior, Springer. . In this Springer Nature encyclopedia entry, Chidichimo reviews why synchronized movement is central to human social life. The article connects ritual synchrony to evolutionary psychology, showing how imitation, over-imitation, and shared movement help humans learn norms, signal belonging, and solve problems of cooperation in large groups. It explains how coordinated action can strengthen affiliation, trust, shared identity, and prosocial behaviour, while also noting risks such as conformity and aggression. Religious and secular rituals appear as key examples of a broader human capacity: using shared action to bind people into social groups.. Read article : Synchronized Movements and Rituals (opens in a new tab) . . . . . December 2024 . Artificial Intelligence Chatbots Mimic Human Collective Behaviour. He, J. K., Wallis, F. P. S., Gvirtz, A., & Rathje, S. · British Journal of Psychology. . Artificial Intelligence (AI) chatbots, such as ChatGPT, have been shown to mimic individual human behaviour in a wide range of psychological and economic tasks. Do groups of AI chatbots also mimic collective behaviour? If so, artificial societies of AI chatbots may aid social scientific research by simulating human collectives. To investigate this theoretical possibility, we focus on whether AI chatbots natively mimic one commonly observed collective behaviour: homophily, people's tendency to form communities with similar others. In a large simulated online society of AI chatbots powered by large language models (N=33,299), we find that communities form over time around bots using a common language. In addition, among chatbots that predominantly use English (N=17,746), communities emerge around bots that post similar content. These initial empirical findings suggest that AI chatbots mimic homophily, a key aspect of human collective behaviour. Read more . J. · PsyArXiv Preprint. . Through one correlational study (n = 1,447) and two digital field experiments (n = 494; n = 1,133), the authors test whether following hyperpartisan influencers on Twitter/X drives affective polarization. Incentivising users to unfollow such influencers improved feelings toward the out-party by 23.5% versus control, with effects persisting at least six months, while also increasing exposure to accurate news and boosting feed satisfaction without reducing engagement.. Social Media Behavior Is Associated with Vaccine Hesitancy. Rathje, S., He, J. K., Roozenbeek, J., Van Bavel, J. J., & van der Linden, S. · PNAS Nexus. . Understanding how vaccine hesitancy relates to online behavior is crucial for addressing current and future disease outbreaks. We combined survey data measuring attitudes toward the COVID-19 vaccine with Twitter data in two studies (N1 = 464, N2 = 1,600 Twitter users) to examine how real-world social media behavior is associated with vaccine hesitancy in the US and UK. We found that following US Republican politicians or hyper-partisan/low-quality news sites was associated with lower vaccine confidence — even controlling for political ideology and education. Network analysis revealed that low and high vaccine confidence participants separated into distinct communities, with centrality in the more right-wing community negatively associated with vaccine confidence in the US but not the UK. In Study 2, likelihood of not getting vaccinated was associated with sharing and favoriting low-quality news on Twitter. Altogether, vaccine hesitancy is associated with following, sharing, and interacting with low-quality information online, illustrating the challenges of encouraging vaccine uptake in a polarized social media environment.
