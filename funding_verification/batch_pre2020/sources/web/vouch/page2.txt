Maybe your lead investor just asked for a Certificate of Insurance (COI) . Maybe a Fortune 500 procurement team flagged your policy limits before signing your first enterprise contract. Either way, insurance just became a deal item.. Startup business insurance is a coordinated set of policies that protects your company, its leaders, and the people doing business with it from financial loss. The coverage you need, and what investors and enterprise buyers expect, changes at every funding stage .. A pre-seed company signing its first SaaS contract has very different risks than a Series C company with a 401(k), Fortune 500 customers, and a board full of outside directors. This guide breaks down what coverage startups typically need at each stage, what it costs, and what can happen when a deal arrives before your insurance does.. Key Takeaways . The type and amount of insurance your company needs changes as you raise, hire, and sign larger customers.. Directors & Officers (D&O) Insurance is the most common investor requirement before wiring seed funds, and most institutional investors require it.. D&O coverage should scale with the total capital you've raised, not just your funding round's name. Companies that keep raising without revisiting their limit are often more exposed than they realize.. Waiting until a deal requires coverage creates real risk: it can delay a close, cost you an enterprise contract, or leave you personally exposed.. Same-day quotes are available when you work with the right broker.. What Is Startup Business Insurance? . Startup business insurance is a bundle of policies that protects the company, its founders, its board, its investors, and its customers from financial loss. It isn't one policy but a coordinated stack of coverage types, each addressing a different category of risk.. That stack does more than pay out after something goes wrong, though. It's also how you prove, quickly, that you've thought about risk before an investor or enterprise buyer has to ask. That's what's actually happening when your lead investor requests a Certificate of Insurance (COI) before wiring funds: they're checking whether the right policies are already in place, not introducing a new requirement out of nowhere.. Why Do Startups Need Business Insurance? . The coverage question rarely comes up as "do I need this?" It shows up as a legal requirement, an investor condition, or a contract clause from an enterprise buyer, and by then, the only thing that matters is whether you're ready.. When Is Business Insurance Required? . Most insurance isn't legally required, but you'll often need to have it in order to close a round or start working with a client.. Institutional investors typically require D&O Insurance before closing most seed rounds. This protects board members and officers personally, and investors know it.. Enterprise contracts routinely require General Liability Insurance and Errors & Omissions (E&O) Insurance , often at specific minimum limits. Procurement teams check for these before a contract is executed.. Commercial leases almost universally require General Liability Insurance as a condition of tenancy.. State law requires Workers' Compensation in nearly every state once you have employees.. What Can Happen If You're Not Covered? . The financial risks that hit earliest are rarely the ones founders prepare for first. Employment lawsuits are the most common financial shock for early-stage companies. The average claim costs approximately $75,000 to settle and over $200,000 if it goes to trial. That's a company-altering number when runway is short.. Cyber risk isn't a large-company problem either. Cyberattacks hit small and mid-sized businesses far more often than large enterprises , and even a modest breach carries real costs in legal fees, notification requirements, and lost trust. Wire fraud is the top financial crime targeting early-stage companies, and a single successful attack can be unrecoverable.. According to SVB's founder insurance guide , two of every three repeat founders make insurance an integral part of their early growth plans. First-time founders rarely give it more than a passing thought. Repeat founders have seen what happens without it.. What Insurance Does My Startup Need at Each Funding Stage? . Vouch works with YC companies, Tier 1 VC-backed startups, and companies closing rounds from Series A through later growth stages every day. The policy stacks below reflect what founders actually need at each stage: what investors require before wiring, what enterprise buyers expect to see on a COI, and what state law mandates as you hire.. What Insurance Do You Need at Pre-Seed? . At pre-seed or while bootstrapping, your exposure is relatively limited. You may not have institutional investors or employees yet, and your contracts are probably smaller. . But two policies can become relevant very early:. General Liability is required if you're signing a commercial lease, joining an accelerator, or working in any shared office space. General Liability covers third-party bodily injury and property damage claims.. Cyber Insurance is appropriate the moment you're collecting any user data, whether that's email addresses, payment information, or behavioral data. Even a small breach triggers notification requirements and legal exposure.. What can usually wait: D&O, Employment Practices Liability Insurance (EPLI) , and Workers' Compensation may not be necessary yet. They'll become more relevant as you raise, hire, and sign larger contracts.. What Changes When You Raise a Seed Round? . Raising institutional capital changes your insurance needs, especially when it comes to D&O.. Most institutional seed investors will require D&O coverage before wiring funds because it protects directors and officers against certain claims arising from decisions they make in those roles.. D&O covers the personal liability of company officers and directors for decisions made in their roles, including claims of mismanagement, breach of fiduciary duty, and securities violations.. Crime Insurance is increasingly required at the seed stage as well. It covers losses from fraud, employee theft, and wire transfer fraud . Early-stage companies are frequent targets since they're moving capital regularly, often without the internal controls that larger organizations have in place. This one is worth having before you need it.. When investors ask for proof of coverage, they'll typically want a COI showing specific limits. They may also ask to be named as additional insureds where applicable.. What Coverage Should You Add at Series A? . Series A is where insurance requirements can compound quickly. You're adding board members, growing your team, and often signing larger enterprise customers, all of which introduce new exposures.. Your insurance stack may now include:. D&O: Limits often increase as the company raises more capital. Board members may specify minimum limits of $2M to $5M. Learn more about D&O insurance costs by company size and stage.. E&O: E&O covers claims that your product or services caused a customer financial loss. Enterprise procurement teams commonly ask for it before signing. Budget: $4,000 to $12,000 per year.. EPLI: EPLI covers claims involving wrongful termination, harassment, discrimination, and other employment-related disputes. It becomes increasingly important as your team grows.. Workers' Comp: It's legally required in nearly every state once you have employees. If you haven't already put coverage in place, you'll need to get current.. What New Risks Emerge at Series B? . By Series B, your company is getting more complex. Enterprise customers may demand higher liability limits, your benefits stack may include a 401(k), and your growing brand can create new media and intellectual property exposure.. That brings several additional policies into the conversation:. Umbrella and Excess Liability cover claims that exceed the limits on your underlying policies. Enterprise contracts may require $5M or more in combined liability coverage, and Umbrella coverage can help you reach those limits.. Fiduciary Liability becomes relevant once you offer a 401(k) or another qualified retirement plan. It covers certain claims involving mismanagement of employee benefit plans.. Media Liability covers certain IP infringement , defamation, and advertising injury claims, which can become more relevant as your brand and content operation grow.. Key Person Insurance is sometimes required by lenders or investors, particularly when one or two founders represent a disproportionate share of the company's value.. How Should Coverage Evolve at Series C and Beyond? . At Series C and later, the question isn't simply which policies you need. It's whether your limits have kept pace with the size and complexity of the company.. This is where gaps can emerge: a company may continue raising capital and signing larger contracts without making corresponding changes to its insurance program. At this stage, total capital raised and actual exposure can be more useful guides than the name of your latest round.. D&O limits should track capital raised. As a rough guide, companies with $0 to $5M raised may carry a $1M limit. At $6M to $20M raised, $2M to $3M is common. At $20M to $60M, that may rise to $3M to $5M. Companies north of $60M raised may consider $5M or more. If your last D&O review happened before your latest round, revisit your limit.. Side A DIC coverage becomes more relevant. It sits on top of your primary D&O program and helps protect individual board members' personal assets , separate from company-level coverage. Boards may increasingly ask about it as you add outside directors.. Enterprise E&O and Cyber limits may continue climbing. A $5M combined limit that satisfied one enterprise customer may not meet another's requirements. Limits of $5M to $10M or more become increasingly common at this stage.. Pricing at Series C and beyond varies too widely by exposure, headcount, and limit selection to generalize. A startup-focused broker can model your specific needs instead.. What Happens If You Don't Have Insurance When You Need It? . Being uninsured tends to become a problem at exactly the wrong time: while you're closing a round, trying to clear procurement, or responding to a claim.. Here are three ways that can play out:. Your funding close gets delayed. Your lead investor requests a Certificate of Insurance before wiring. You don't have one. Now you're shopping for coverage under deadline pressure while the close waits.. An enterprise contract gets held up. A Fortune 500 procurement team requires $2M in E&O coverage. You don't have it, so they can't approve the vendor relationship until you do.. An employment claim hits your runway. A former employee files a wrongful termination claim. Without EPLI, you're paying legal defense costs out of pocket (potentially a significant expense for a startup with limited runway).. Learn more about why tailored coverage creates better business protection .. What Startup Insurance Mistakes Should You Avoid? . Insurance gets complicated quickly as a startup grows. The biggest problems often aren't caused by having no insurance at all, but by carrying coverage that no longer matches the business.. Watch for these common mistakes:. Waiting until a deal requires coverage. Shopping under deadline pressure gives you less time to compare options and understand policy terms. Build coverage before you need to prove you have it.. Underinsuring D&O relative to capital raised. A $1M D&O policy may work early on but become insufficient as you raise more capital. Revisit your limit after each round.. Buying from a generic broker. Standard small-business policies may contain exclusions that don't fit technology companies, digital products, or investor-related claims. Work with a broker that understands your business model.. Not naming additional insureds correctly. Investors and enterprise customers may require additional insured status on specific policies. Make sure your COI reflects the contractual requirement.. Not updating coverage after a round. Funding, headcount, valuation, and contract size can all change your exposure. Review your policies after each close, not just at renewal.. Confusing LLC protection with insurance. An LLC can limit personal liability in certain circumstances, but it doesn't replace coverage for employment claims, client lawsuits, cyber incidents, or director-level liability.. How Do You Get Startup Business Insurance? . Getting covered doesn't need to be complicated. The key is to understand what you'll need before an investor, customer, or landlord asks you to prove you have it.. Here's a straightforward process:. Identify what your stage requires. Use the framework above, or our step-by-step founder's guide , to identify what you need now and what you're likely to need over the next 12 months.. Work with a startup-focused broker. Look for someone who understands technology companies, investor requirements, and enterprise contract language.. Request certificates of insurance immediately. Once coverage is bound, get your COIs so they're ready when investors and enterprise customers ask.. Review coverage at major milestones. Revisit your insurance after funding rounds, significant hiring, and new enterprise contracts.. Learn more about the process so you know what to gather, what to ask, and what to watch out for before you bind a policy.. Keep Your Coverage in Step With Your Growth . Your insurance should grow with your company. Raising capital, hiring employees, adding board members, and signing larger customers can all change the policies and limits you need. The best time to review those changes is before an investor or customer discovers a gap for you.. Closing a round, signing an enterprise customer, or overdue for a coverage review? Talk to a Vouch advisor . We work with startups from pre-seed through growth and understand the coverage investors and customers expect.. Frequently Asked Questions . Do I need business insurance before I raise money?. You may not be required to have every policy in place before raising. General Liability and Cyber coverage can become relevant earlier, while D&O is commonly required by institutional investors as part of a seed financing.. What insurance does a VC require before investing?. Institutional investors commonly require D&O before wiring seed funds, and some may also require Crime / Fidelity coverage. At Series A, investors and new board members may specify D&O limits of $2M to $5M and review more of your insurance program during diligence. At later stages, appropriate limits increasingly depend on your total capital raised and broader exposure.. How much does startup business insurance cost?. A typical seed-stage SaaS company with 10 employees may spend $12,000 to $20,000 per year for a stack that includes General Liability, D&O, Cyber, and Crime. Your actual cost will depend on factors including funding, headcount, revenue, claims history, location, and the limits you choose.. How much D&O coverage do I need at Series B or later?. Size it to your total capital raised, not your headcount or round name. As a general guide, $1M is typically enough up to about $5M raised, $2M to $3M covers most companies between $6M and $20M raised, $3M to $5M fits $20M to $60M raised, and companies north of $60M raised should be looking at $5M or more. If you haven't revisited your limit since your last round closed, that's usually the first place to check.. What is Side A DIC coverage, and do I need it?. Side A DIC (Difference in Conditions) coverage sits on top of your primary D&O program and protects individual board members' personal assets in certain situations where the company's primary coverage may not respond. It becomes more relevant as companies add outside and independent directors, particularly at Series C and beyond. It's worth discussing with your broker as your board evolves.. What's the difference between D&O and General Liability?. General Liability covers third-party claims for bodily injury and property damage, and is required for leases and most physical business activities. D&O covers the personal liability of company officers and board members for decisions made in their leadership roles, including investor claims and governance disputes. Most companies need both since they cover completely different risks .. Can I get startup insurance fast?. Working with a startup-focused broker, coverage can typically be bound in 24 to 48 hours, and a COI issued the same day. Two weeks gives you time to approach the process properly. Starting now gives you more room to review the coverage instead of rushing the day before closing.. Is my LLC enough protection?. It may not be. An LLC can limit certain types of personal liability, but it doesn't replace insurance for employment-related lawsuits, customer claims, cyber incidents, or certain liabilities involving directors and officers. Your business structure and insurance program address different risks and work alongside each other.. When should I update my coverage?. Review your coverage after every funding close, significant headcount milestone, and new enterprise contract with insurance requirements. Your exposure changes as your company grows.
