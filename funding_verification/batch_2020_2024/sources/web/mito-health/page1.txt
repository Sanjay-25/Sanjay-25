If you have ever felt tired for no clear reason, struggled with brain fog, or noticed unusual hair loss, your iron stores might be running low. More specifically, your ferritin levels could be low, and that matters more than most people realize.. At Mito Health, we believe understanding your bloodwork is the first step toward taking control of your energy, performance, and long-term health. This guide covers everything you need to know about ferritin: what it is, what normal ferritin levels look like, the symptoms of low ferritin, how to interpret your results, and proven ways to raise your ferritin level — through food, supplements, IV iron, and addressing the root cause. We will also cover the less-talked-about flip side: when ferritin is too high.. What Is Ferritin?. . Ferritin is a protein that stores iron in your body. You can think of it like a savings account for iron — there when your body needs it for things like making red blood cells, supporting your brain, and carrying oxygen to your muscles.. Most of your iron is locked inside hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. The rest is held in reserve, mostly bound to ferritin in your liver, spleen, bone marrow, and skeletal muscle. When iron intake or absorption falls short, your body draws down this reserve. Serum ferritin, the level measured in a blood test, is the single best marker of how full those reserves are. For the full reference, including normal ferritin ranges by sex and what high and low results mean, see the ferritin biomarker overview .. A small amount of ferritin also circulates freely in the blood. That circulating ferritin is what gets measured on your lab report, and it correlates closely with the larger pool sitting in your tissues. Roughly 1 ng/mL of serum ferritin reflects about 8–10 mg of stored iron in adults. So if your ferritin reads 30 ng/mL, your total reserve is approximately 240–300 mg of iron — barely a one-month buffer if you stop replacing what you lose. A healthy ferritin of 100 ng/mL represents closer to 800–1000 mg in reserve, which is why functional medicine care team lead push for the higher end of normal.. Why ferritin matters: Ferritin can drop long before hemoglobin does, which means you can be iron-deficient and feel awful while your standard iron panel still looks “normal.” This is called non-anemic iron deficiency, and it is one of the most under-diagnosed causes of fatigue, hair loss, and exercise intolerance — particularly in menstruating women, athletes, and vegetarians.. How ferritin fits into the iron panel: a complete iron evaluation includes serum iron, ferritin, transferrin, transferrin saturation, and total iron-binding capacity. Each tells a different story. Serum iron varies by the hour and meal; ferritin is the storage marker; transferrin is the transport protein; saturation is the working ratio of the two. When all five are read together, the diagnosis usually becomes obvious — and the most common pattern in early deficiency is low ferritin with still-normal hemoglobin and serum iron.. Most U.S. labs use ranges anchored to outdated population averages, which often miss functional iron deficiency. Optimal vs. normal — why the gap matters: Many labs flag a ferritin of 15 or 20 ng/mL as “normal.” Functional medicine care team lead and a growing body of research disagree. Symptoms of iron insufficiency — fatigue, hair shedding, restless legs, exercise intolerance — frequently appear well before ferritin drops below the standard cutoff. Studies show energy, cognition, and athletic performance often do not fully recover until ferritin sits comfortably above 50 ng/mL, with many experts targeting 70 to 100 ng/mL for active people and women with heavy periods.. A ferritin under 30 ng/mL almost always reflects depleted stores even if a CBC has not yet caught it. A ferritin above 200 ng/mL in someone without inflammation deserves a closer look — see the High Ferritin section below.. Ranges that change with life stage: ferritin needs are not static. A premenopausal woman losing 20–30 mg of iron per cycle has a much higher daily turnover than a postmenopausal woman or an adult man. Pregnancy roughly doubles iron requirements (to ~27 mg/day in the second and third trimesters), which is why even a “borderline” first-trimester ferritin of 30 ng/mL is now considered insufficient by the British Society for Haematology and the WHO. Endurance athletes lose iron through sweat, gut microbleeding, and footstrike hemolysis at rates 30–70% above sedentary peers, and many sports medicine care team lead target ferritin above 50 ng/mL just to avoid performance decline.. How often to test: if your ferritin is in the optimal range, an annual check is usually enough. If you are actively repleting, recheck at 8 and 16 weeks. If you are at high risk — heavy periods, pregnancy, vegetarian or vegan diet, regular blood donor, endurance athlete, history of GI disease — testing twice a year is reasonable.. Low Ferritin Symptoms (The Complete Checklist). . Low ferritin starves your tissues of oxygen-carrying capacity and disrupts the production of dopamine, serotonin, and thyroid hormone. The symptoms are wide-ranging and often dismissed as “just stress” or “getting older.” If you check more than three of the boxes below, book a health coach consult for a ferritin test.. Persistent fatigue and weakness — the most common symptom, present in over 60% of low-ferritin cases even without anemia. . Hair shedding or thinning — diffuse hair loss is one of the earliest signs in women; ferritin under 40 ng/mL is strongly linked to telogen effluvium. . Brittle, ridged, or spoon-shaped nails (koilonychia). . Restless legs syndrome (RLS) — the urge to move your legs at night; sleep-medicine guidelines recommend evaluating iron status in people with RLS and consider iron repletion when ferritin is below 75 ng/mL. . Cold hands and feet — poor peripheral circulation and impaired thyroid hormone conversion. . Dizziness or lightheadedness on standing. . Headaches , especially in the morning. . Brain fog and difficulty concentrating . . Shortness of breath with mild exertion. . Exercise intolerance — slower recovery, higher heart rate at submaximal effort. . Pica — unusual cravings for ice (pagophagia), starch, dirt, or paper; pagophagia is so specific to iron deficiency it is considered diagnostic. . Sore or smooth tongue (atrophic glossitis), cracks at the corners of the mouth (angular cheilitis). . Mood changes — anxiety, low mood, irritability tied to disrupted dopamine synthesis. . Heart palpitations or a racing pulse. . If you are losing more hair than usual , fatigued despite sleeping well, or notice ice cravings, run a ferritin test before assuming the cause is something else.. Symptoms by ferritin level: the symptoms above tend to appear in a fairly predictable order as ferritin falls. Restless legs, ice cravings, and exercise intolerance often emerge first, sometimes when ferritin is still in the 30–50 ng/mL range. Hair shedding usually appears below 40 ng/mL. Fatigue and brain fog become unmistakable below 30 ng/mL. Pallor, dizziness, breathlessness, and a fast resting heart rate suggest ferritin under 15 ng/mL with developing anemia. The wide variation between individuals is real — some people stay symptomatic at 40 ng/mL while others feel fine at 25 ng/mL — but the ordering of symptoms tends to hold.. Is Your Ferritin Low? Low ferritin often means your iron stores are depleted, even if your hemoglobin is still in the normal range. This is called non-anemic iron deficiency, and it is more common than you would think — affecting an estimated 1 in 5 menstruating women globally.. Symptoms of low ferritin may include:. Fatigue and weakness. . Brain fog or difficulty concentrating. . Dizziness or shortness of breath. . Headaches. . Brittle nails or hair loss. . Feeling cold all the time. . Beyond the symptoms list, sustained low ferritin has measurable downstream effects: it impairs thyroid hormone conversion (T4 to T3), reduces dopamine and serotonin synthesis, weakens immune cell function, and slows wound healing. In children, even mild iron insufficiency is linked to cognitive delays. In adults, restoring ferritin to the 70–100 ng/mL range is associated with measurable improvements in energy, mood, and exercise capacity within 8–12 weeks.. The takeaway: when your ferritin level is too low, it can affect your energy, your mental clarity, your immune system, and even your exercise performance.. Why Are Some People More Prone to Low Ferritin?. . Some people simply need more iron than others, or they have trouble absorbing it. You are more likely to have low ferritin if:. You are a menstruating woman, especially with heavy periods. . You are pregnant or have recently given birth. . You follow a vegetarian or vegan diet. . You have gut issues like celiac disease, H. pylori infection, or IBS. . You have had a gastric bypass or other surgeries affecting digestion. . You donate blood regularly. . You have chronic inflammation (which can block iron absorption). . You take long-term acid-suppressing medication (PPIs or H2 blockers), which reduces iron absorption. . You are an endurance athlete — both training and footstrike hemolysis deplete iron. . The good news? No matter the cause, there are practical steps you can take toward raising ferritin levels — and Mito Health can help you personalize that plan.. How to Raise Ferritin Levels Safely and Effectively. 1. Eat More Iron-Rich Foods. The best way to raise ferritin levels naturally is to start with your diet. Your body absorbs iron in two forms: heme and non-heme. Heme iron, which is found in animal-based foods, is the most easily absorbed. This includes red meat like beef or lamb, organ meats like liver, poultry, and seafood such as oysters, clams, and tuna.. Non-heme iron is found in plant foods like lentils, beans, tofu, spinach, kale, and fortified cereals. Even though it is not as easy for your body to absorb as heme iron (which comes from animal sources), you can boost its absorption by eating these foods with something high in vitamin C, like oranges, bell peppers, or strawberries.. Just a tip: try to steer clear of tea, coffee, or calcium-rich foods when you have iron-rich meals, since they can make it harder for your body to take in the iron.. 2. Use the Right Supplements. Sometimes, food alone is not enough, especially if your ferritin is already low. In that case, iron supplements can help you rebuild your stores more efficiently. Common forms include ferrous sulfate, ferrous gluconate, and ferrous bisglycinate.. The dose matters too. While high doses are often prescribed, research shows that taking lower doses — around 45 mg of elemental iron — every other day may actually lead to better absorption and fewer side effects.. To improve effectiveness, take your supplement with vitamin C and on an empty stomach, if possible. Just be cautious about taking it alongside calcium or high-fiber meals, which can reduce how much iron your body absorbs. If you experience side effects like constipation, nausea, or cramping, you can book a health coach consult to switch to a gentler option.. 3. Fix the Root Cause. Raising ferritin levels is not just about putting more iron in — it is also about stopping the loss.. That might mean:. Treating heavy periods. . Screening for gut conditions like celiac disease or H. pylori. . Managing chronic inflammation or autoimmune disease. . Getting a referral for gastrointestinal testing if you are losing blood somewhere unseen. . 4. Monitor and Recheck. Once you are working on how to raise your ferritin level, do not forget to follow up. It usually takes 3 to 6 months of consistent changes to fully rebuild your iron stores.. The ideal target? Most experts recommend keeping ferritin above 70 to 100 ng/mL for optimal energy, immune support, and recovery, especially for active people, menstruating women, and those with chronic health conditions.. 5. Pair Iron with the Right Co-Factors. Iron does not work in isolation. Several nutrients influence how efficiently your body absorbs, transports, and uses iron:. Vitamin C — converts non-heme iron from its ferric to ferrous form, where it is absorbed 2–3x more efficiently. Absorption studies typically paired roughly 100 to 200 mg of vitamin C with a non-heme iron source to produce that effect.. . Vitamin A — required to mobilize stored iron out of the liver. Vitamin A deficiency is a frequent under-recognized cause of “iron-resistant” anemia.. . Copper — essential for ceruloplasmin, the enzyme that loads iron onto transferrin. Long-term high-dose zinc can trigger functional copper deficiency and stall iron repletion.. . Vitamin B12 and folate — both are needed to produce healthy red blood cells. A combined deficiency is common in vegetarians and can mask or coexist with iron deficiency.. . Riboflavin (B2) — supports iron mobilization and hemoglobin synthesis; deficiency reduces ferritin response to iron supplementation.. . Best Foods High in Iron and Ferritin. Dosing strategy backed by recent research: the Stoffel et al. trials found that taking 60 mg of elemental iron every other day (rather than 60 mg daily) produced higher cumulative absorption and lower hepcidin spikes, and reduced nausea and constipation. In those trials the iron was taken on an empty stomach with about 250 mg of vitamin C, and separated by at least an hour from coffee, tea, calcium, and antacids, all of which blunt non-heme iron absorption. Your prescribing clinician can help you set a dose and schedule that fits your labs and tolerance.. How long to supplement: recheck ferritin at 8–12 weeks. Most people need 3–6 months of consistent supplementation to fully replenish stores. Stopping too early is the most common reason ferritin rebounds back to baseline.. Side effects and how to manage them: the most common complaints are constipation, dark stools, nausea, and a metallic taste. Switching from sulfate to bisglycinate resolves symptoms for most people. Splitting the dose in half, taking with a small amount of food, or moving to alternate-day dosing can also help. Liquid forms (ferrous sulfate elixir, iron-protein succinylate solution) are useful for children or anyone who cannot swallow tablets. Avoid enteric-coated or extended-release iron — they reduce absorption without proportional gains in tolerability.. What about lactoferrin or curcumin-based “iron support”? Lactoferrin (a milk-derived protein) modestly improves iron absorption in some studies, particularly in pregnancy, and it has fewer GI side effects than ferrous sulfate. Evidence is mixed — it is reasonable as an adjunct, not a replacement. Curcumin-based “ferro-curc” products are early-stage; they look promising for tolerability but have not been compared head-to-head with bisglycinate.. When to Consider IV Iron. IV iron skips the gut entirely, which makes it the right choice when oral iron will not work or work fast enough. It is increasingly used in primary care, not just hematology — and modern formulations (ferric carboxymaltose, iron isomaltoside, ferric derisomaltose) are far safer than the older iron dextran.. Consider IV iron if: . Severe deficiency — ferritin under 15 ng/mL with anemia or significant symptoms, especially before surgery or pregnancy. . Failed oral iron — three months of compliant oral supplementation without ferritin response. . Intolerable side effects from oral iron despite trying multiple forms. . Malabsorption — celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, post–bariatric surgery, chronic H. pylori. . Ongoing significant blood loss — heavy menstrual bleeding, GI bleeding, dialysis. . Second- or third-trimester pregnancy with iron-deficiency anemia, when fast correction is needed. . Chronic kidney disease, heart failure, or chemotherapy-related anemia — settings where IV iron has the strongest evidence. . A typical IV course delivers 500–1,000 mg of iron in one or two infusions, with ferritin rising substantially within 2–4 weeks. Side effects are uncommon but can include transient flushing, nausea, or — rarely — hypersensitivity reactions, which is why infusion happens in a monitored clinical setting.. How IV iron compares to oral: oral iron typically raises hemoglobin by 1 g/dL over 4–6 weeks; IV iron does the same in 2–3 weeks and replenishes ferritin much faster. The cost differential is real (a single ferric carboxymaltose infusion runs several hundred to over a thousand dollars in the U.S.), so insurance authorization usually requires documented oral iron failure or a qualifying condition. Outside the U.S., IV iron is increasingly first-line for moderate-to-severe iron-deficiency anemia, particularly in pregnancy.. What to expect at the appointment: infusions take 15–60 minutes depending on the formulation. You will be monitored during and for 30 minutes after. Most people feel no different that day; some experience a mild flu-like reaction (joint aches, low-grade fever) for 24–48 hours after, which is benign and self-limiting. Iron stores typically rise within 1–2 weeks, with peak ferritin response at 4–6 weeks. Recheck labs at that point — if ferritin remains low, repeat infusion is sometimes needed.. High Ferritin: When Too Much Is a Problem. Most ferritin conversations focus on the low end, but high ferritin is also a meaningful signal. Iron is one of the few minerals your body cannot actively excrete — once it is in, it stays in. Persistent high ferritin can reflect harmless inflammation, but it can also indicate serious iron overload.. Common causes of high ferritin: . Chronic inflammation — ferritin rises with infection, autoimmune disease, obesity-driven inflammation, and metabolic syndrome. Pair with CRP to distinguish.. . Hereditary hemochromatosis — a genetic condition (most often HFE C282Y mutation) that causes the gut to over-absorb iron. Suspect if ferritin > 300 ng/mL with transferrin saturation > 45%. Affects roughly 1 in 200 people of Northern European descent.. . Liver disease — fatty liver, alcohol-related liver disease, and chronic hepatitis all elevate ferritin. . Frequent blood transfusions — transfusional iron overload. . Excessive iron supplementation — taking iron without confirmed deficiency. . Why it matters: Untreated iron overload deposits iron in the liver, heart, pancreas, and joints — leading to cirrhosis, cardiomyopathy, diabetes, and arthritis. Treatment is straightforward (therapeutic phlebotomy or iron chelation), but only if the diagnosis is made early. Anyone with persistently high ferritin and elevated transferrin saturation deserves an HFE gene test.. How to tell inflammation apart from true overload: . Inflammation pattern: high ferritin, normal or low transferrin saturation, elevated CRP, normal liver enzymes. Treat the underlying inflammation; ferritin will normalize.. . Iron overload pattern: high ferritin, transferrin saturation > 45%, normal CRP, often elevated liver enzymes. Order an HFE genotype test and consider liver MRI to quantify hepatic iron.. . Mixed pattern: obesity-related metabolic syndrome can produce both — moderate ferritin elevation with mildly elevated saturation. Weight loss and exercise often correct it.. . If you take iron supplements: never supplement iron without a recent ferritin and transferrin saturation result. Routine supplementation in someone who is iron-replete can drive ferritin into overload territory within months — particularly in men, postmenopausal women, and people with undiagnosed HFE mutations. The “iron-fortified multivitamin daily for years” pattern is a real cause of incidental iron overload.. Key Takeaways.
