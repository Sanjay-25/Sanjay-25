I can hear, but I can't understand. People tend to lose high frequency hearing first. Patients with severe-to-profound high-frequency hearing loss often complain they can hear but cannot understand, even with the most powerful hearing aids. That is because they have lost the ability to hear the sounds that are vital to understanding speech.. Hear the sounds. sss shh fff . . . . . Current treatments for hearing loss leave a gap. . . Hearing aids. Hearing aids provide acoustic amplification, making sound louder, and can work well for mild to moderate hearing loss.. With severe or profound sensorineural hearing loss, hair cells inside the cochlea may be too damaged to convert mechanical vibrations into neural signals effectively. Making sound louder may no longer be enough.. THE GAP. . . . Cochlear implants. Cochlear implants bypass damaged or dysfunctional hair cells to electrically stimulate auditory neurons within the cochlea.. Implantation typically involves drilling through the mastoid bone behind the ear to access the cochlea and insert an electrode array.. The procedure can cause significant, permanent loss of residual hearing in the implanted ear. Some patients retain useful natural hearing, while others lose it entirely.. . . . For patients that fall in the gap. Everyday conversations become harder. For some people with severe high-frequency hearing loss, hearing aids offer limited benefit, leaving everyday speech difficult to understand.. Cochlear implants may help, but concerns about surgery and losing remaining hearing can make people reluctant to proceed.. The ongoing struggle can be isolating. Hearing loss is associated with depression and an increased risk of dementia.. Sources NIDCD: hearing aids and the limits of amplification . FDA: benefits and risks of cochlear implants . Electrical stimulation across the cochlear wall . . . A novel approach to hearing restoration. Auricle is developing a minimally invasive neural implant with the goal of restoring high-frequency hearing with minimal risk to residual hearing.. By avoiding disruption of the internal structure of the cochlea, our extracochlear approach aims to be significantly less invasive and reversible.. . . . . Potential impact.
