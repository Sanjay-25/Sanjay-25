In this article. . Title. . . . . . . . 9 minutes read. . . . Aug 19, 2026 . . . . What is a lawyer retainer. A lawyer retainer is an upfront fee a client pays to secure an attorney's services. The attorney holds the funds in a client trust account, often called an Interest on Lawyers' Trust Account (IOLTA), until earning them by performing legal work. Think of it as a deposit that creates a formal attorney-client relationship and guarantees the lawyer will be available when the client has legal needs.. When someone says they have a "lawyer on retainer," they typically mean they've paid an upfront fee for ongoing access to legal counsel. This differs from hiring an attorney for a single matter, like reviewing one contract or handling one lawsuit. The retainer arrangement establishes a working relationship that's already in place when issues come up.. One distinction worth noting: the retainer fee and the retainer agreement are two different things. The fee is the actual money deposited into trust. The agreement is the written contract that governs everything else, including how fees get billed, when the client tops up the balance, and what happens to unused funds at the end.. Types of retainer fees. Retainers come in several varieties, and the type determines how funds are held, when they're considered earned, and whether the client can get money back.. Earned retainer fee. An earned retainer is money the attorney has already earned through completed work. Once the attorney performs services and bills for them, funds move from the client trust account to the attorney's operating account. At that point, the fee becomes non-refundable because it compensates for work already done.. Unearned retainer fee. An unearned retainer is an upfront payment deposited into trust before any work begins. The funds stay the client's property until the attorney performs work and bills against them. This is the most common type, and it's what most people picture when they hear "retainer.". General retainer fee. A general retainer, sometimes called an availability retainer, is a fee paid to guarantee the attorney's availability over a set period. Unlike other retainers, it's not tied to specific legal work. The client is paying the attorney to reserve capacity and be ready when called upon. General retainers are often non-refundable because they compensate the lawyer for turning away other work.. Special retainer fee. A special retainer is payment to retain an attorney for a specific case or matter rather than general availability. It's tied to a defined scope of representation with clear boundaries around what legal work is covered.. Evergreen retainer fee. An evergreen retainer includes an automatic replenishment clause. When the balance drops below a set threshold, the client is required to top it back up to the original amount. The retainer billing cycle follows a predictable pattern from start to finish.. 1. The client pays the retainer and funds are held in trust. The client deposits the retainer fee into the attorney's client trust account, typically an IOLTA. The funds remain the client's property until earned. Attorneys have an ethical obligation to keep trust funds separate from operating funds, and commingling is prohibited under bar rules in every state.. 2. The attorney bills time against the retainer balance. As the attorney works on the client's matters, they billable hours and deduct fees from the trust balance. Accurate, contemporaneous time tracking is critical here. Vague entries like "research" or entries submitted days after the work was done can lead to billing disputes. The balance shrinks as work progresses.. 3. The client receives itemized billing statements. Firms typically send monthly invoices detailing how the retainer was used. A good statement breaks down activity by date, timekeeper, hours, and description of work performed. Transparency at this stage builds client confidence in the arrangement.. 4. The retainer is replenished or remaining funds are refunded. Two outcomes are possible when the balance runs low or the matter ends. If the retainer drops below a threshold specified in the agreement, the client deposits additional funds. If the matter concludes with unused funds remaining, the attorney is ethically required to return the balance promptly, usually within a timeframe set by state bar rules.. Common activities billed against a retainer include:. Legal research. . Phone calls and client communication. . Court appearances. . Document drafting and review. . Correspondence with opposing counsel. . How much does it cost to retain a lawyer. Retainer amounts vary widely depending on several factors, and there's no universal standard.. Practice area and complexity: A straightforward contract review requires less upfront funding than complex commercial litigation.. . Attorney experience and reputation: More experienced attorneys typically command higher rates and larger retainers.. . Geographic location: Legal fees vary significantly by market, with major metropolitan areas tending toward higher costs.. . Anticipated scope and duration: Matters expected to require substantial work over many months call for larger retainers.. . Fee structure: Some retainers are based purely on hourly rates, while others include flat-fee components for specific tasks.. . The retainer amount is typically negotiable. It reflects a reasonable estimate of initial work, and clients can discuss fee structure, billing rates, and replenishment terms before signing anything.. What to include in a lawyer retainer agreement. A retainer agreement, sometimes called an engagement letter, is the formal contract governing the attorney-client relationship. A clear agreement prevents misunderstandings and protects both parties.. Scope of legal services. The agreement defines exactly what legal work the retainer covers and what falls outside scope. Clear boundaries here prevent scope creep and billing disputes later.. Fee structure and billing rates. This section specifies the attorney's hourly rate, blended rates if multiple timekeepers will work on the matter, any flat-fee components, and how rates may change over time.. Billing frequency and reporting. How often will the client receive itemized statements? Monthly is standard. The agreement also addresses what level of detail will appear in billing narratives.. Payment terms and replenishment thresholds. For evergreen retainers, this section specifies the minimum balance that triggers a replenishment request, the amount to be replenished, and the timeline for payment.. Trust account disclosures. The agreement explains where funds will be held, how trust accounting is managed, and the client's right to an accounting of trust activity at any time.. Termination and refund provisions. How can either party end the agreement? What happens to the retainer balance upon termination? What's the timeline for returning unused funds? These provisions work together and are typically addressed in the same section.. Retainer fees compared to other legal fee structures. Retainer arrangements are most common for ongoing matters like business law, family law, and complex litigation, where the scope of work is uncertain and the client benefits from having counsel available on a continuing basis.. Why law firms use retainer fees. From the firm's perspective, retainers serve several practical functions.. Cash flow predictability: Retainers provide upfront funding that smooths out revenue and reduces reliance on collections.. . Client commitment: A retainer signals the client is serious about the engagement and invested in the outcome.. . Reduced collections risk: Billing against funds already in trust is far more reliable than invoicing after the fact and hoping for payment.. . Ethical compliance: Trust accounts are required for holding client funds, and retainers ensure proper trust accounting from day one.. . Resource allocation: Knowing funds are available allows firms to staff matters confidently without worrying about whether they'll get paid.. . Clients benefit too. Priority access, predictable costs, and built-in budgeting make retainer arrangements attractive for those with ongoing legal needs.. Best practices for managing retainer billing at your firm. For law firms, retainer billing is only as good as the underlying time data. A few practices make a significant difference in outcomes.. 1. Track time accurately against retainer balances. Retainer billing depends entirely on the quality and timeliness of time entries. Late entries, vague descriptions, or missing time altogether leads to under-billing and revenue leakage , or alternatively, to client disputes when entries seem inflated or unclear. Capturing time as work happens, rather than reconstructing it days later, produces more accurate and defensible bills.. 2. Communicate retainer usage to clients regularly. Proactive, detailed billing statements build trust and reduce disputes. Itemizing by activity type, date, and timekeeper shows clients exactly where their money went. Waiting for the client to ask questions is a recipe for friction.. 3. Use technology to automate retainer billing and compliance. Modern legal billing software can automate time capture , enforce billing guidelines, and generate compliant invoices, reducing manual effort and errors. Firms using AI-powered tools can catch compliance issues before invoices go out rather than scrambling to fix problems after a client rejects a bill..
